\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

We evaluate \sys{} on a heterogeneous Jetson testbed around the three axes
introduced in Section~\ref{sec:intro}: \emph{Recovery Latency},
\emph{Recovery-State Footprint}, and \emph{Recovery Feasibility}, together
with \emph{KV-State Fidelity} and the failure-free cost of protection.  The
questions are: (RQ1) how the recovery latency of each family scales with the
failure position $P$; (RQ2) how much recovery state single and double parity
retain relative to replication, and how the gap grows with model size and
context length; (RQ3) what recovery guarantee and state fidelity \sys{}
provides for one and two stage failures; (RQ4) whether joint placement and
backup assignment widens the memory range in which recovery stays feasible;
and (RQ5) what parity protection costs during failure-free decoding.  Every
result is labelled as a \emph{live} fleet measurement, a \emph{controlled
offline} analysis on measured profiles, or a \emph{derived projection} from
model geometry.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:eval:setup}

\textbf{Testbed.}  The coordinator runs on a Jetson AGX Xavier.  Workers are
two Jetson AGX Orin 32\,GB (\texttt{ao-1}, \texttt{ao-2}) and three Jetson
Orin Nano 8\,GB (\texttt{on-1}, \texttt{on-2}, \texttt{on-6}), all executing
stages on CUDA over a shared LAN; two further Orin Nano boards run CPU-only
workers that serve only as candidate backup hosts in the placement study.
The model is OPT-350M in 16-bit floating point (fp16) (24 decoder layers, 16 heads $\times$ 64
dimensions), so each layer appends 4\,096 bytes of KV state per token.  The
recovery experiments use the placement produced by \sys{}'s scheduler:
\texttt{ao-2} holds the head stage (layers 1--15) and the four protected
non-head stages hold nine layers in total, the largest of them four.  A
request is a short prompt decoded greedily for 36 tokens, and the failure
position $P\in\{4,8,16,24,32\}$ is the number of tokens generated before the
failure.

\textbf{Recovery families.}  We compare five recovery paths under one
coordinator and one injection mechanism.  \emph{Recompute} rebuilds the
pipeline and replays the request from position~0 through every stage;
\emph{Petals} replays only the failed layer range on the backup from the
retained stage inputs~\cite{borzunov2023petals}; \emph{DejaVu} installs the
KV replica that every protected stage streamed to the
coordinator~\cite{strati2024dejavu}; \emph{Reconfigure} keeps no backup
($R=\emptyset$), re-solves placement on the surviving workers, cold-loads
weights, rebuilds the pipeline, and replays the request; and \sys{} uses one
parity column ($k=1$) or, for two simultaneous failures, two ($k=2$).
Recompute and Reconfigure are controlled baselines rather than reproductions
of named systems.

\textbf{Failure injection.}  A worker-side fault raises a crash on the victim
while it computes position $P$, after the victim's input for that position
has reached the coordinator, so every trial fails at exactly the intended
token.  Pipeline forwarding is synchronous during these sweeps so that the
failed layer interval propagates through the RPC trailer; asynchronous
forwarding adds a heartbeat-bounded detection delay that is common to all
families and is not part of the recovery work compared here.  The primary
victim is \texttt{on-1} (layers 16--17) with \texttt{on-6} as its assigned
backup; a second sweep fails \texttt{on-6} to test victim sensitivity.
Reconfigure has no assigned backup, so its victim is chosen from the live
placement in each trial and is marked dead explicitly, which also excludes
its detection delay.

\textbf{Metrics and validity.}  \emph{Recovery Latency} is the client-observed
interval from the last token received before the failure to the first valid
token after recovery, i.e., the token-between-time at the failure position.
The same definition applies to every family, including the replay catch-up of
Reconfigure.  \emph{Recovery-State Footprint} is the number of bytes the
coordinator retains per KV token for a request.  \emph{KV-State Fidelity} is
bit identity between the recovered KV bytes and the bytes the failed stage
held.  \emph{Recovery Feasibility} is whether a placement and a recovery
mapping exist together under a per-device memory limit.  A trial is valid
only if the fault fired at $P$, the decoded sequence equals the failure-free
reference, and the intended branch executed (checked through a coordinator
log marker for the parity and replication paths, which otherwise fall back to
replay).  All trials reported below passed these gates; sequence match serves only
as a validity gate.

Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery-latency} and Table~\ref{tab:recovery-summary} answer
RQ1; each marker is one trial, each line the least-squares fit over
$P\in\{4,\dots,32\}$, and the dotted line marks one failure-free decode step.
The two recomputation families scale with the prefix.  Recompute adds
164\,ms per generated token, one full pipeline pass per position, and reaches
5.6\,s at $P=32$.  Petals confines replay to the two failed layers on the
backup and adds 16\,ms per position, ten times less, but its latency still
grows from 0.32\,s to 0.77\,s across the sweep.  \sys{} recovers the failed
stage's KV from the parity column and the surviving stages, so its latency is
0.30\,s at $P=4$ and 0.32\,s at $P=32$: a slope of 0.87\,ms per position,
which is $19\times$ flatter than Petals and $188\times$ flatter than
Recompute.  At $P=32$ the parity path is $2.4\times$ faster than Petals and
$17.8\times$ faster than Recompute, and the gap widens with every additional
token.  DejaVu, which installs a stored replica instead of decoding parity,
has the same flat shape (2.67\,ms per position) and a slightly lower
intercept because it fetches one column rather than the surviving three.  The
two zero-recomputation families are tied in latency, at roughly twice one
decode step; relative to DejaVu, \sys{} offers the same latency with
$2.25\times$ less retained state (Section~\ref{sec:eval:footprint}).

Reconfigure recovers in 24.25\,s at the median (18.62--39.35\,s over the five
positions).  Replay catch-up accounts for 1.6\,s at $P=4$ and 6.4\,s at
$P=32$; the remainder is re-solving placement on the survivors, cold-loading
weights on the newly assigned workers, and rebuilding the pipeline, which
dominates regardless of $P$.  Because the surviving fleet differs per trial,
we report the level and range rather than a slope.  Storing no recovery state
thus costs two orders of magnitude in recovery latency relative to any family
with a pre-assigned backup.

\textbf{Victim sensitivity.}  Failing \texttt{on-6} (layers 18--19) instead
of \texttt{on-1} gives the same ordering, 163, 17.5, and 1.43\,ms per position
for Recompute, Petals, and \sys{}; relative to one decode step, the parity
recovery stays at $1.6$--$1.9\times$ for every $P$ and both victims, whereas
Petals grows from $1.9\times$ to $5.0\times$ and Recompute from $6\times$ to
$34\times$.

\subsection{Recovery-State Footprint and Failure Tolerance}\label{sec:eval:footprint}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{paper/fig_storage_tolerance.pdf}
  \caption{Retained recovery state per KV token versus tolerated
  simultaneous non-head failures $k$ (placement geometry of the measured
  fleet).}
  \label{fig:storage-tolerance}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{paper/fig_storage_scaling.pdf}
  \caption{Retained state saved by single parity relative to replication
  versus context length (derived projection, balanced five-stage pipeline;
  measured OPT-350M point marked).}
  \label{fig:storage-scaling}
\end{figure}

On the measured placement, the four protected stages hold 36\,864 bytes of KV
per token in total and the largest holds 16\,384.  DejaVu retains the sum.
A single parity column retains one padded largest column, 16\,384 bytes, or
$2.25\times$ less, and two columns retain 32\,768 bytes, 11\% less than
replication.  Fig.~\ref{fig:storage-tolerance} generalizes this: $k$ parity
columns cost $k\cdot\ell_{\max}$ while replication costs $\sum_s\ell_s$, so
parity is smaller only while $k<\sum_s\ell_s/\ell_{\max}$, which is 2.25 on
this placement.  A third column would retain more state than replication
while tolerating fewer failures, which is why the implementation stops at
$k=2$; the crossover moves outward on pipelines with more or better balanced
non-head stages.

The per-token difference is small in absolute terms, but backup state
accumulates over the context and grows with the model.  At a 2\,048-token
context the measured placement retains 72\,MB under replication and 32\,MB
under single parity (Fig.~\ref{fig:storage-scaling}); projected to a balanced
five-stage pipeline, OPT-6.7B at 2\,048 tokens retains 819 versus 205\,MB and
OPT-13B at 4\,096 tokens 2.5\,GB versus 640\,MB.  These projections are
arithmetic on layer shape and token footprint, not measurements.

\textbf{Two simultaneous failures.}  With $k=2$, we fail two protected stages
at once (\texttt{on-1} and \texttt{on-6}) at each of the five positions.  All
five trials executed the double-parity branch and produced the failure-free
reference sequence, so both lost KV columns were reconstructed from $P$ and
$Q$ without recomputing the prefix (RQ3).  The fitted slope, 2.78\,ms per
position, is in the same flat band as single parity and DejaVu.  The measured
intercept of 30.3\,s, however, is an artifact of this fleet: the automatically
built recovery mapping assigns every non-head backup to the same Orin Nano,
so a two-stage failure makes that board load a second stage cold before it
can serve.  The intercept is a placement effect that a backup mapping spread over
different hosts avoids: with the 7B fleet's distributed backups the
double-parity intercept is 0.58\,s (Section~\ref{sec:eval:scale}), and the
same 350M fleet recovers a single failure in 0.28\,s.  We therefore use the
350M double-parity result only for its slope and its guarantee, and do not
compare its absolute latency with the other families.

\subsection{KV-State Fidelity}\label{sec:eval:fidelity}

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \scriptsize
  \caption{Cross-tier replay of one protected stage: regenerated KV compared
  element-wise with the original (live probe).}
  \label{tab:fidelity}
  \begin{tabular}{l r}
    \toprule
    KV elements differing between tiers & 26.9\% \\
    Maximum absolute difference & $2^{-8}$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The parity and replication paths install the bytes that were stored, so a
completed parity recovery is bit-exact by construction; the unit tests assert
layer-wise identity of the recovered K and V tensors, and the live trials
confirm the branch executed.  Replay-based recovery regenerates KV through a
model forward pass, and on heterogeneous hardware that pass need not
reproduce the original bytes.  Table~\ref{tab:fidelity} quantifies this with a
controlled probe: the same fp16 input forwarded through the same two-layer
stage (layers 16--17, eight positions) on \texttt{on-1} (CUDA) and on a
CPU-only Orin Nano worker differs in 26.9\% of KV elements, with a maximum
absolute difference of $2^{-8}$, consistent with kernel-dependent reduction
order and accumulation precision rather than a defect.  We present fidelity as
a determinism property of the recovered state: parity and
replication guarantee it whenever their primary path completes, whereas
replay fallbacks, Petals, Recompute, and Reconfigure regenerate a KV cache
whose bytes depend on the backup's hardware tier.  The probe covers one tier
pair and one stage; same-tier drift and deeper stages remain to be measured.

\subsection{Recovery Feasibility}\label{sec:eval:feasibility}

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \scriptsize

RQ4 asks whether solving placement and backup assignment together admits
deployments that a sequential procedure loses.  With the uncapped fleet
(5.4--25\,GB free per device) both procedures succeed, so the live
deployments above cannot show coupling.  We keep the measured layer profiles,
compute times, and link profiles of the fleet and cap each device's free
memory at $c$, sweeping $c$ from 6\,GB down to 200\,MB.  The
\emph{cost-first} procedure runs the placement DP with $R=\emptyset$ and then
hands the result to the production backup solver; the \emph{joint} procedure
runs the alternating search of Section~\ref{sec:design:dp}.  Both enumerate the same ordered subsets of the
five CUDA workers.  Backup hosts are either the pipeline devices
(the coordinator's default) or the whole fleet, which adds two CPU-only Orin
Nano boards that never earn a stage but hold 5--6\,GB.

Table~\ref{tab:feasibility} gives the result.  Restricted to pipeline
devices, cost-first placement stops admitting a backup at $c=600$\,MB, while
joint placement remains feasible down to $c=300$\,MB; below 250\,MB no
placement of the 601\,MB model (25\,MB per layer) has room for backups on any
procedure.  The
mechanism is visible in the placements.  At $c=600$\,MB the cost-only DP
prefers two stages, \texttt{on-1}[1--23]$\to$\texttt{on-6}[24], because
fewer hops minimize the bottleneck stage; the 23-layer stage weighs 576\,MB,
and no peer can reserve that beside its own stage.  The joint solver charges
the reservation to the next placement and returns four stages,
\texttt{on-1}[1--21]$\to$\texttt{on-6}[22]$\to$\texttt{ao-2}[23]$\to$
\texttt{ao-1}[24], whose largest stage has a host.  At $c=300$\,MB it spreads
the model over all five workers with at most six layers each so that every
stage's backup fits beside the backup host's own stage.  Widening the backup
scope to the whole fleet moves both thresholds down, since the CPU nodes
absorb reservations, but the same gap reappears at $c=200$\,MB, where
cost-first placement has no backup for the four-layer AGX stage and joint
placement reorders the pipeline to find one.  These are feasibility results
under a memory constraint, not serving-latency results; they show that the
backup reservation in Eq.~\ref{eq:mem} changes which placements are
admissible once memory is tight, which is the regime that memory-constrained
IoT deployments occupy.

\subsection{Cost of Protection}\label{sec:eval:cost}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{paper/fig_protection_cost.pdf}
  \caption{Failure-free cost of protection relative to protection off (live,
  OPT-350M; mean and sample standard deviation over three rounds).}
  \label{fig:protection-cost}
\end{figure}

Protection is paid for during every failure-free decode step, because each
protected stage ships its new KV column to the coordinator.  We measure it on
the same placement with protection off, single parity, double parity, and
replication, three rounds each in interleaved order; all 2\,400 token
intervals come from requests that completed 20 tokens with identical greedy
output across modes.  Fig.~\ref{fig:protection-cost} reports the result
(RQ5).  With protection off the pipeline decodes 5.24\,tok/s with a median
token interval of 183\,ms.  Single parity reduces throughput by
$5.1\pm2.4$\% and raises the median token interval by $5.8\pm2.6$\%; double
parity costs $6.3\pm0.3$\% and $7.3\pm0.5$\%; replication costs
$6.3\pm1.2$\% and $7.2\pm0.7$\%.  Double parity and replication are
indistinguishable (4.906 versus 4.906\,tok/s): both ship the same KV columns,
and the second GF($2^8$) column is folded at the coordinator, away from the
workers' forward path.  Tolerating a second failure with parity costs one
more retained column and no additional decode-time overhead.  Network traffic
itself is not an evaluation axis in this paper.

To test whether these findings survive model scale, we repeated the recovery
experiments with OPT-6.7B in fp16 --- 19$\times$ more parameters, 32 decoder
layers, and 16.4\,kB of KV state per layer per token --- served as a
six-stage pipeline by six CUDA workers of the same testbed (the five above
plus a fourth Orin Nano), with a failure-free median token interval of
515\,ms.  Table~\ref{tab:scale-7b} summarizes the same
measurement protocol at this scale.  Recomputation becomes steeper with the
model: Recompute's slope grows 2.8$\times$ (164.3 to 453.9\,ms per position),
while \sys{}'s slope remains statistically zero, so every \sys{} recovery
costs the equivalent of two to three failure-free decode steps
(0.86--1.55\,s) regardless of failure depth, and DejaVu's recovery often
disappears into step jitter outright.
The zero-recompute storage gap also widens with depth: replication retains
426\,kB per token against \sys{}'s 115 (ratio 3.7 versus 2.25 at 350M).

Three further observations close loops left open at 350M.  First, with the
7B placement's backups landing on distinct nodes, double-parity recovery
posts a 0.58\,s intercept and completes in 0.53--1.24\,s across depths ---
direct evidence that the 30.3\,s intercept in
Table~\ref{tab:recovery-summary} was placement concentration, not algorithm
cost.  Second, Reconfigure's median rises to 439.4\,s (413.1 and 465.8\,s at
the two measured positions), dominated by re-profiling and cold redeployment
of 12.4\,GiB of weights: the price of holding no recovery state grows
roughly 18$\times$ from 350M.  Third, protection stays cheap as the model
grows: over the same three-round interleaved protocol, single parity costs
$0.6\pm10.7$\% of throughput (within measurement noise), double parity
$3.9\pm3.5$\%, and replication $7.6\pm2.4$\%.  The scheduler's live solve at
this scale also exhibits the feasibility coupling of
Section~\ref{sec:eval:feasibility} directly: 496 of 1\,950 candidate
placements were rejected by backup memory provisioning alone, and the
offline cap sweep repeated on the 7B cluster profile finds the decoupled
place-then-protect procedure infeasible at \emph{every} per-device memory
cap at which the model fits (6.5\,GB up to the fleet's actual free memory),
while the joint solve succeeds throughout --- at this scale the production
operating point itself lies inside the coupling band.
