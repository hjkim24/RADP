\subsection{Setup}\label{sec:eval:setup}

\textbf{Fleet.} Eight Jetson workers (six Orin Nano 8\,GB, one AGX
Orin in MAXN power mode, one AGX Xavier) plus a Mac-hosted
coordinator on the same LAN. Workers span three compute tiers we
calibrate per-layer at seq$=$64 (the decode-step regime, not
prefill): CPU on Nano ($\sim$42\,ms/layer), CPU on AGX
($\sim$17.6\,ms/layer), and CUDA on Nano ($\sim$1.5\,ms/layer). AGX
Orin MAXN at the same seq$=$64 decode point is roughly
16$\times$ faster per layer than Nano CUDA --- a gap that did not
appear in our earliest measurements because two silent profiler
bugs (tokenizer padding returning the wrong shape and
\texttt{perf\_counter()} timing around async CUDA kernels) hid the
AGX advantage behind launch overhead. After commit~382739b fixes
both, AGX dominance is consistent across thirty repeats.

\textbf{Models.} OPT-350M (24 decoder layers, hidden 1024, fp16) is
our primary model: small enough to fit a Nano even at the layer
floor, large enough to expose the heterogeneity gap. To probe
model-size generalisation we run a 3$\times$-larger Llama-3.2-1B
(16 layers, hidden 2048, fp16) on the same fleet via the sharded-
safetensors loader (\S\ref{sec:design:runtime}). OPT-1.3B's
monolithic \texttt{.bin} format and Llama-3.2-3B's 6\,GB working
set both exceed Nano memory and are orthogonal to our contribution
(\S\ref{sec:disc}).

\textbf{Workload.} Greedy decoding (temperature$=$0);
\texttt{max\_tokens} varies by experiment (30 for the concurrent
throughput sweep, 60 for recovery trials). Concurrency points use
\texttt{measure\_\allowbreak{}concurrent.py}; recovery trials use
\texttt{run\_phase3\_\allowbreak{}recovery.py}. Every reported cell
is backed by a JSON artifact under \texttt{experiments/results/},
catalogued in the repository's \texttt{REPORT.md} for
reproducibility.

\subsection{$\psi$+$R$ joint optimization beats decoupled baselines in normal mode}\label{sec:eval:a3b}

On a 7-worker 3-tier fleet (3 CPU-Nano, 3 CPU-AGX, 1 CUDA-Nano)
we compare \sys{}'s joint $(\psi,R)$ DP against three placement-
only baselines that share an identical recovery substrate
($R{=}\emptyset$, abort on first failure): \emph{greedy}
(layers handed out fastest-device-first), \emph{uniform} (equal
layer count per stage), and \emph{Jupiter-DP} ($\alpha{=}1$
throughput-optimal). Each cell is 30 TBT samples per stream
$\times$ 10 streams ($n{=}300$).

\sys{} ($\alpha{=}0$, latency-optimal) beats greedy by 6.5\% on TBT
p50, 3.8\% on TBT p95, and 8.3\% on aggregate tokens-per-second.
The interesting part is where the gap comes from. The cost model
predicts greedy and \sys{} should tie at a 45.3\,ms max-stage
budget; the live gap exists because the cost function does not
charge pipeline-transition overhead (gRPC framing plus
Python/GIL contention on consecutive small stages), and \sys{}'s
fewer-bigger-stage solutions exploit that overhead asymmetry while
greedy's many-small-stage solutions absorb it. This
predicted-vs-measured discrepancy bounds the claim:
$\psi$+$R$ optimization captures everything the cost model
captures, but extracts an additional 2--4\% from runtime mechanics
the cost model under-counts. We revisit the asymmetry in
\S\ref{sec:disc}.

\subsection{Failure recovery survives where baselines abort}\label{sec:eval:recovery}

\textbf{Star topology.} Five trials kill a non-coordinator worker
mid-stream after 17--20 tokens have been issued (the harness is
\texttt{run\_phase3\_\allowbreak{}recovery.py} with the
\texttt{--kill\allowbreak-after\allowbreak-token N} flag).
\sys{} delivers all 60 expected tokens coherently in 5/5 trials;
all three placement-only baselines abort the stream at the kill
boundary, losing the 17--20 in-flight tokens already returned to
the client. Recovery wall-clock is tight: mean 617\,ms, p95
670\,ms, a $\sim$100\,ms spread driven by the lazy weight transfer
to the promoted backup. The mirror-cache cost is negligible per
step (one one-way RPC per non-head stage, mean 1.2\,ms) and the
arithmetic checks out exactly: an 8-token Generate produces 16
mirror pushes ($8$ steps $\times$ 2 non-first stages of a 3-stage
chain).

\textbf{Chain topology.} The 4-stage chain
\texttt{ao-1[1..9]} $\to$ \texttt{on-6[10..14]} $\to$
\texttt{on-1[15..19]} $\to$ \texttt{on-2[20..24]} isolates a
\emph{mid-chain} failure (on-6), which exercises the
trailer-based attribution path; head/tail kills would resolve via
heartbeat alone. With sync chain forwarding, all 60 tokens land
coherently; the coordinator journal shows the upstream worker
catching the downstream RpcError, stamping the failed range
\texttt{(10,14)} onto the gRPC trailer, and the coordinator looking
that range up in the \emph{original} placement to identify on-6 as
the truly-dead worker --- this is essential because heartbeat has
already substituted ao-1 as the backup by then. With async chain
forwarding, trailer metadata is unavailable (the response chain
unwound long before failure), so attribution falls back to the
chain head while heartbeat catches up; the $\psi$+$R$ feasibility
guarantee preserves correctness (all 60 tokens delivered), but
recovery pays one extra hop when heartbeat loses the race.

\subsection{Latency placement dominates throughput placement at C $\geq$ 4 across two model sizes}\label{sec:eval:matrix}

Table~\ref{tab:matrix} is the headline result: aggregate throughput
(tok/s, higher is better) and TBT p50 (ms, lower is better) across
a 24-cell OPT-350M matrix (two chain lengths $\times$ four
architecture variants $\times$ three concurrency levels) and a
nine-cell Llama-3.2-1B sub-matrix on the same 4-stage fleet. Four
deltas drive the discussion.

\textbf{(i) L $\succ$ T at every one of the 24 OPT-350M cells.}
Pairwise comparison within sync (T+sync vs L+sync) and within async
(T+async vs L+async) shows L wins on aggregate throughput at every
chain length and every concurrency level we measured. The biggest
gap is at the 4-stage chain $C{=}4$ async cell, where L+async
(23.9\,tok/s) is 2.4$\times$ T+async (9.8\,tok/s) and TBT p50 is
2.8$\times$ lower (145\,ms vs 406\,ms): T-placement's even split
gives every stage one or two layers, so the chain saturates on
network hops with the per-stage compute idle, while L-placement
concentrates compute on the AGX worker until pipeline parallelism
actually has work to overlap.

\textbf{(ii) async $\succ$ sync everywhere at $C\geq 4$, except
T-placement on the 4-stage chain.} On every L-placement row at
$C\geq 4$, async beats sync (e.g. 3-stage $C{=}4$ L: 24.25 $\to$
33.71\,tok/s, $+39\%$; 4-stage $C{=}4$ L: 18.66 $\to$ 23.91,
$+28\%$). The single regime where async \emph{loses} is T-placement
on the 4-stage chain at $C{=}4$ (10.71 $\to$ 9.79\,tok/s, $-9\%$):
chain-hop overhead per stage becomes a larger fraction of total
cost when each stage has only one or two layers to compute,
swamping async's overlap budget. This nuance bounds the claim:
async chain forwarding helps when the per-stage compute is large
enough to hide a one-hop ACK, which is exactly the regime
L-placement creates.

\textbf{(iii) async gain is chain-length-independent.} At $C{=}16$,
T-placement sync$\to$async is $+47\%$ on the 3-stage chain
(23.49 $\to$ 34.49\,tok/s) and $+46\%$ on the 4-stage chain
(24.47 $\to$ 35.69\,tok/s). The cost of synchronous chain
forwarding scales with concurrency, not with chain length: $C$
streams each occupy one gRPC handler thread per stage until the
nested response unwinds, so adding a stage adds $C$ more occupied
slots, but async chain breaks the dependency entirely.

\textbf{(iv) Model-size generalisation, with a C{=}1 caveat.} On
the 3$\times$-larger Llama-3.2-1B model (4-stage chain, three
captured cells $\times$ three C levels $=$ nine points), async
$\succ$ sync persists in the T-placement row ($C{=}16$:
21.84 $\to$ 30.56\,tok/s, $+40\%$) and L $\succeq$ T persists at
$C\geq 4$ (L+async@C{=}4 is the best Llama cell, $+12\%$ over
T+async; at $C{=}16$ they tie within 0.6\%). The L+sync cell is
absent because one Nano went unreachable mid-sweep; the three
captured cells still bound the claim. The new observation is at
$C{=}1$: T+async beats L+async on aggregate throughput
(3.74 vs 3.05\,tok/s, $-18\%$) for the first time in any cell.
TBT p50 still favors L (174 vs 261\,ms, $-33\%$), so the
operator-facing interpretation is unchanged --- L wins
responsiveness --- but the strict throughput dominance does not
generalise to single-stream big-model regimes. The intuition:
when stage compute dwarfs chain-hop cost (bigger model, single
in-flight stream), L's fast-device-heavy placement makes that
single stage the throughput-limiting critical path while T's
even division keeps every device busy; multi-stream amortises the
hop and L's pipeline-saturation advantage returns.

\textbf{Operating point span.} The best $C{=}16$ cell on this
fleet is OPT-350M 4-stage L+async at 41.65\,tok/s; the worst is
OPT-350M 3-stage T+sync at 23.49\,tok/s. The $+77\%$ throughput
swing is covered entirely by two runtime knobs
(\texttt{optimization\_mode} and \texttt{chain\_mode}) that the
operator can flip without re-deploying.

\textbf{Caveat: stage count is not a free knob.} 3-stage L+async at
$C{=}4$ delivers 33.71\,tok/s while the same cell on 4-stage drops
to 23.91 ($-29\%$). The 4-stage chain forces one Nano to host a
single layer (the layer floor), paying a full network hop to do
$\sim$1.5\,ms of compute. Recall that \sys{}'s DP assumes every
device in the fleet participates (\S\ref{sec:design:dp}), so the
stage count is fixed by the fleet size $|D|$ at boot, not chosen
by the DP. The two chain-length topologies in this matrix were
produced by curating the fleet inventory --- removing two workers
to go from 4-stage to 3-stage --- so the same DP, run twice over
two different fleets, produced the rows above. The takeaway is
that operators steer the system into the right regime by
curating the fleet first, and the DP optimises placement and
recovery on top of that choice.

\subsection{Subset selection and hop overhead do not reverse L $\succ$ T}\label{sec:eval:cost-model}

We test the natural cost-model hypothesis for the matrix's
L $\succ$ T gap --- T loses because the DP forces every worker
in and because wire-only $T_{\mathrm{comm}}$ understates per-hop
overhead --- by toggling both fixes on a 6-worker fleet
(1 AGX MAXN, 3 Nano CUDA, 2 Nano CPU), OPT-350M~async. Subset
enumeration (Eq.~\ref{eq:dp}) drops both CPU workers; L picks a
2-device subset (AGX$+$Nano) and T picks the 4-stage all-CUDA
subset, and the L/T throughput ratio \emph{widens} to
$1.21\times$/$1.57\times$/$1.15\times$ at $C\!=\!1/4/16$. Adding
$\gamma_{\mathrm{hop}}\!=\!8$\,ms leaves T at 4-stage 4-CUDA ---
a uniform $\gamma_{\mathrm{hop}}$ does not reorder the max-min
argmax --- and yields $5.17/7.5/32.8$\,tok/s, $5\%$ worse at
$C\!=\!16$ than (a)-only T. The residual gap is a multi-stream
system-level effect (thread-pool contention, Nano KV bandwidth,
gRPC handler occupancy) no wire-cost term reaches; the
Eq.~\ref{eq:rank} multiplier $\mu(\psi)$ and additive
$\gamma_{\mathrm{stage}}|\psi|$ expose these factors, with
$\gamma_{\mathrm{stage}}\!>\!0$ required to break $\mu$'s
homogeneous-fleet tie. Live calibration of
$(C_\star, P, \gamma_{\mathrm{stage}})$ remains future work.
An offline sweep across the full
$(C_\star, \gamma_{\mathrm{stage}})$ grid on the live cluster's
profile snapshot reveals a sharper finding about the production
4-stage T placement itself: the cost-only DP picks a 2-stage
\texttt{on-1[1..23]$\to$ao-1[24..24]} answer for every v2
setting we tested, yet the live coordinator deploys 4 stages.
The forcing factor is Eq.~\ref{eq:mem} --- the 2-stage answer
puts a 23-layer backup burden on a single Nano peer that
cannot also absorb the other devices' backups --- making this
the cleanest empirical instance of the $\psi$+$R$ coupling
claim from \S\ref{sec:intro}: the 4-stage T topology is the
max-min argmax \emph{under} the memory-feasibility constraint,
not without it.
