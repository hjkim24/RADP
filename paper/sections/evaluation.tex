\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

We evaluate \sys{} on a heterogeneous Jetson testbed around the three axes
introduced in Section~\ref{sec:intro}: \emph{Recovery Latency},
\emph{Recovery-State Footprint}, and \emph{Recovery Feasibility}, together
with \emph{KV-State Fidelity} and the failure-free cost of protection.  The
questions are: (RQ1) how the recovery latency of each family scales with the
failure position $P$; (RQ2) how much recovery state single and double parity
retain relative to replication, and how the gap grows with pipeline depth and
context length; (RQ3) what recovery guarantee and state fidelity \sys{}
provides for one and two stage failures; (RQ4) whether joint placement and
backup assignment admits deployments that placement followed by backup
assignment loses; and (RQ5) what parity protection costs during failure-free
decoding.  Every result is labelled as a \emph{live} fleet measurement, a
\emph{controlled offline} analysis on measured profiles, or a \emph{derived
projection} from model geometry.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:eval:setup}

\textbf{Testbed and model.}  The coordinator runs on a Jetson AGX Xavier.
Workers are two Jetson AGX Orin 32\,GB (\texttt{ao-1}, \texttt{ao-2}) and
four Jetson Orin Nano 8\,GB (\texttt{on-1}, \texttt{on-2}, \texttt{on-5},
\texttt{on-6}), all executing stages on CUDA over a shared LAN.  The model is
OPT-6.7B in 16-bit floating point (fp16): 32 decoder layers, 32 heads
$\times$ 128 dimensions, 12.4\,GiB of weights, so each layer appends
16\,384 bytes of KV state per token and no Orin Nano can hold the model
alone; either AGX Orin could, so on this fleet the pipeline is the
scheduler's throughput choice and capacity binds on the Nanos.  \sys{}'s scheduler places it, under its
throughput objective, as a six-stage pipeline:
\texttt{on-6} holds the head stage (layers 1--6) and the five protected
non-head stages hold 26 layers, \texttt{on-2} (7--10), \texttt{on-1}
(11--14), \texttt{on-5} (15--18), \texttt{ao-2} (19--25), and \texttt{ao-1}
(26--32), the largest of them seven layers; the scheduler's recovery mapping
assigns every stage a backup on another node.  A request is a 19-token prompt decoded
greedily for 36 tokens, the failure position $P\in\{4,8,16,24,32\}$ (a
scalar, distinct from the parity column $P[r,q]$) is the number of tokens
generated before the failure, and one \emph{decode step}
below denotes the median failure-free token interval of the same sweep,
500\,ms at 7B and 163\,ms at 350M.  Every (family, position) pair is
measured in three independent trials, each on a freshly restarted pipeline,
so each fit pools 15 trials and carries a standard error.

\textbf{Small-model microscopy.}  The cross-tier fidelity probe, the
five-position Reconfigure sweep, and the analysis of a concentrated backup
mapping need byte-level instrumentation or repeated boots that the 7B
deployment makes impractical; for these we use OPT-350M in fp16 (24 layers,
4\,096 bytes of KV per layer per token) on a five-stage pipeline over five of
the same workers (\texttt{ao-2} head, layers 1--15; \texttt{on-1} 16--17;
\texttt{on-6} 18--19; \texttt{ao-1} 20--23; \texttt{on-2} 24; 163\,ms median token
interval in the recovery sweep), plus two CPU-only Orin Nano workers as candidate backup hosts in
the placement study.  Both deployments share the coordinator, the injection
mechanism, and the validity gates, and every 350M result is marked.

\textbf{Recovery families.}  Five recovery paths run under one coordinator
and one injection mechanism: \emph{Recompute} replays the request from
position~0 through every stage; \emph{Petals} replays only the failed layer
range on the backup from the retained stage
inputs~\cite{borzunov2023distributed}; \emph{DejaVu} installs the KV replica
that every protected stage streamed to the
coordinator~\cite{strati2024dejavu}; \emph{Reconfigure} keeps no backup
($R=\emptyset$), re-solves placement on the survivors, cold-loads weights,
rebuilds the pipeline, and replays; and \sys{} uses one parity column
($k=1$) or two ($k=2$).
Petals and DejaVu name our re-implementations of those systems' recovery
mechanisms on this coordinator, and Recompute and Reconfigure are controlled
baselines rather than reproductions of named systems.

\textbf{Failure injection.}  A worker-side fault raises a crash on the victim
while it computes position $P$, after the victim's input for that position
has reached the coordinator, so every trial fails at exactly the intended
token.  Forwarding is synchronous during these sweeps so that the failed
layer interval propagates through the RPC trailer; the heartbeat-bounded
detection delay of asynchronous forwarding is common to all families and not
part of the recovery work compared here.  The primary victim is
\texttt{on-2} (layers 7--10) with \texttt{on-1} as its assigned backup; a
second sweep on the same placement fails \texttt{on-5} (layers 15--18).
Reconfigure has no assigned backup, so its victim is chosen from the live
placement in each trial and marked dead explicitly.

\textbf{Metrics and validity.}  \emph{Recovery Latency} is the client-observed
interval from the last token received before the failure to the first valid
token after recovery, i.e., the token-between-time at the failure position.
The same definition applies to every family, including the replay catch-up of
Reconfigure.  \emph{Recovery-State Footprint} is the number of bytes retained per KV
token for a request beyond the pipeline itself: KV replicas or parity at the
coordinator, and the per-position stage inputs that failed-stage replay
keeps.  The backup weights that $R$ preloads (a second copy of every stage, the
head included, 12.4\,GiB across this fleet at 7B) are common to every family with a
pre-assigned backup and are accounted in Recovery Feasibility, not here.  \emph{KV-State Fidelity} is
bit identity between the recovered KV bytes and the bytes the failed stage
held.  \emph{Recovery Feasibility} is whether a placement and a recovery
mapping exist together under a per-device memory limit.  A trial is valid
only if the fault fired at $P$, the decoded sequence equals the failure-free
reference, and the intended branch executed (a coordinator log marker for the
parity and replication paths, which otherwise fall back to replay); all
trials below passed these gates.

Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery-latency} and Table~\ref{tab:recovery-summary} answer
RQ1; each marker is one trial, each line the least-squares fit over
$P\in\{4,\dots,32\}$, and the dotted line marks one failure-free decode step.
The two recomputation families scale with the prefix.  Recompute adds
$447\pm6$\,ms per generated token, one full pipeline pass per position, and
grows from 3.1\,s at $P=4$ to 15.7\,s at $P=32$, 31 decode steps.  Petals
confines replay to the four failed layers on the backup and adds
$43\pm3$\,ms per position, ten times less, but its latency still grows from
1.07\,s to 2.31\,s across the sweep.  \sys{} recovers the failed stage's KV
from the parity column and the surviving stages, so its latency is 1.32\,s at
$P=4$ and 1.42\,s at $P=32$: a fitted slope of $5\pm3$\,ms per position,
indistinguishable from zero over the measured range, against 43 for Petals
and 447 for Recompute.  Reconstruction is linear in the prefix (one byte-wise
XOR per position, Algorithm~\ref{alg:cross-stage-parity}), so the slope is
the per-position cost of that loop; the fit bounds it below 12\,ms per
position (two standard errors), and we do not extrapolate beyond $P=32$.
At every measured depth a \sys{} recovery costs the equivalent of two to
three failure-free decode steps (2.1--3.2$\times$ the median token interval,
with a per-position spread of 0.03--0.23\,s across trials).  Its fixed cost
is higher than Petals' (1.23 versus 0.85\,s), so the two fits cross at
$P\approx10$: below ten generated tokens Petals recovers faster; at $P=32$
\sys{} is $1.6\times$ faster than Petals and $11\times$ faster than
Recompute, and the gap widens with every additional token.  DejaVu, which installs a stored
replica instead of decoding parity, has the same flat shape ($-6\pm3$\,ms per
position, within two standard errors of zero and not modelled) and an intercept 0.28\,s lower (0.95 versus 1.23\,s): it installs
one column held at the coordinator, whereas \sys{} first fetches the four
surviving columns from their workers, four RPC round trips, before the XOR.  Our DejaVu baseline keeps the
replica at the coordinator, whereas the published design streams it to a
peer node.
At $P=32$ the difference is 0.65\,s, about one decode step.  Relative to
DejaVu, \sys{} trades that step for $3.7\times$ less coordinator state
(Section~\ref{sec:eval:footprint}).

Reconfigure recovers in 465.8 and 413.1\,s at $P{=}4$ and $8$
(mean 439.4\,s).  In the logged trial, re-profiling the five survivors took
about 250\,s and cold-loading and rewiring the redeployed stages about
130\,s; the remainder is re-solving and the replay itself.  All of this is
independent of $P$, and because the surviving fleet differs per trial we
report levels rather than a slope.  Holding no recovery state thus
costs more than two orders of magnitude relative to any family with a
pre-assigned backup, and the penalty grows with the model: on the 350M
deployment the same baseline recovers in 24.25\,s at the median
(18.62--39.35\,s over five positions).

\textbf{Victim sensitivity.}  Failing \texttt{on-5} (layers 15--18) instead
of \texttt{on-2} on the same placement (one trial per position) gives the
same ordering: 462, 48, and $-4.0$\,ms per position for Recompute, Petals,
and \sys{}.  Relative to one decode step, the parity recovery stays within
$2.1$--$3.9\times$ for every $P$ and both victims, whereas Petals grows from
$1.5\times$ to $4.7\times$ and Recompute from $5\times$ to $32\times$.  The 350M deployment reproduces
the ordering at its own scale with two victims (164 and 163\,ms per position
for Recompute, 16 and 17.5 for Petals, 0.87 and 1.43 for \sys{}).

\subsection{Recovery-State Footprint and Failure Tolerance}\label{sec:eval:footprint}

On the measured placement, the five protected stages hold 425\,984 bytes of
KV per token in total (416\,kB) and the largest holds 114\,688 (112\,kB).
DejaVu retains the sum; a single parity column retains one padded largest
column, $3.7\times$ less, and two columns retain 224\,kB, 46\% less than
replication.  Both families retain the tail stage's column, whose recovery
falls back to replay (Section~\ref{sec:disc}); over the four stages the
parity path recovers, the ratio is $2.7\times$.  Petals retains instead the
fp16 input of every protected stage at every position, 40\,kB per token here.
Parity and replication ship the same KV columns from the workers, so their
difference is coordinator memory, not traffic.  In general, $k$ parity
columns cost $k\cdot\ell_{\max}$ while replication costs $\sum_s\ell_s$, so
parity is smaller only while $k<\sum_s\ell_s/\ell_{\max}$, 3.71 on this
placement and 2.25 on the head-heavy 350M placement, where a third column
would already retain more state than replication; the implementation stops
at $k=2$.  Over a 2\,048-token context the 7B placement retains 832\,MB
under replication and 224\,MB under single parity (350M: 72 versus 32\,MB),
a derived projection from the measured layer shapes.

\textbf{Two simultaneous failures.}  With $k=2$, we fail two protected
stages at once (\texttt{on-2} and \texttt{ao-2}, whose backups
\texttt{on-1} and \texttt{ao-1} sit on distinct nodes), three trials at each
of the five positions.  All 15 trials executed the double-parity branch and
produced the failure-free reference sequence, so both lost KV columns were
reconstructed from $P$ and $Q$ without recomputing the prefix (RQ3), in
1.13--1.24\,s on average per position, with an intercept of $1.13\pm0.08$\,s
and a slope of $3.5\pm3.9$\,ms per position; the intercept sits 0.11\,s
below single parity's, within the fit uncertainty.  With the two backups on
distinct nodes, two failures cost the same as one.  The absolute cost depends on where
the backups land.  On the 350M deployment the scheduler assigned every
non-head backup to the same Orin Nano, and the same two-failure trials, while
reconstructing both columns at every position with a slope of 2.78\,ms per
position, posted an intercept of 30.3\,s because that board had to load a
second stage cold before it could serve.  The intercept is a placement
effect, and the distributed mapping at 7B removes it.

The parity and replication paths install the bytes that were stored, so a
completed parity recovery is bit-exact by construction (the unit tests assert
layer-wise identity of the recovered K and V tensors).  Replay-based recovery
regenerates KV through a model forward pass, which on heterogeneous hardware
need not reproduce the original bytes.  Table~\ref{tab:fidelity} quantifies this with
a controlled probe on the 350M deployment, the only one whose stages fit a
CPU-only board (a tier pair absent from the all-CUDA 7B fleet): the same fp16 input forwarded through the same two-layer
stage (layers 16--17, eight positions) on \texttt{on-1} (CUDA) and on a
CPU-only Orin Nano differs in 26.9\% of KV elements, with a maximum absolute
difference of $2^{-8}$, consistent with kernel-dependent reduction order and
accumulation precision.  Fidelity is thus a determinism property of the
recovered state: parity and replication guarantee it whenever their primary
path completes, whereas replay fallbacks, Petals, Recompute, and Reconfigure
regenerate KV whose bytes depend on the backup's hardware tier.  The probe
covers one tier pair and one stage.

RQ4 asks whether solving placement and backup assignment together admits
deployments that a sequential procedure loses.  At 7B the answer is visible
in the live solve itself: of the 1\,950 ordered device subsets the scheduler
enumerated, 496 were rejected solely because no peer could reserve the backup
weights that Eq.~\ref{eq:mem} charges beside its own stage.  To locate the
regime, we keep the measured layer, compute, and link profiles of each fleet
and cap every device's free memory at $c$, $M(d)\leftarrow\min(M(d),c)$,
over 40 caps from 0.2 to 23\,GB.  The \emph{cost-first} procedure
runs the placement DP with $R=\emptyset$ and then hands the result to the
production backup solver; the \emph{joint} procedure runs the alternating
search of Section~\ref{sec:design:dp}.  Both enumerate the same ordered
subsets of the six CUDA workers.  Backup hosts are either the pipeline devices
(the coordinator's default) or the whole fleet, which lets a device that
earns no stage hold backups.

Table~\ref{tab:feasibility} gives the result.  For OPT-6.7B, with backups
restricted to pipeline devices, cost-first placement finds no backup at
\emph{any} cap at which the model fits, from 4.8\,GB up to the fleet's
uncapped free memory (3.1--5.8\,GB on the Nanos, 23\,GB on the AGX boards
before deployment), while joint placement is feasible throughout: the
production operating point of this deployment lies inside the coupling
band.  The cost-only DP concentrates large stages on the two AGX boards and
leaves no peer able to reserve the largest stage's weights beside its own,
whereas the joint solver charges the reservation to the next placement and
spreads the stages.  A headroom rule does not substitute for the joint
search: placing with every device's free memory scaled by 0.75 or 0.5 and
then reserving on the true capacities still finds no backup at any cap in
this scope.  Allowing backups on non-pipeline devices dissolves the gap,
since a device that holds no stage absorbs the reservations, and both
procedures then succeed down to 4.8\,GB, although the headroom variants
fail earlier (0.75 below 6.5\,GB, 0.5 below 5\,GB) because a shrunken
capacity packs stages onto fewer devices.  The 350M deployment shows the
same structure only under synthetic caps: with 5--25\,GB free per device
both procedures succeed, and the joint-only band appears at 300--600\,MB
(pipeline devices) and 200\,MB (whole fleet, where two CPU-only nodes absorb
reservations).
These are feasibility results under a memory constraint and say nothing
about serving latency; they show that the backup reservation in Eq.~\ref{eq:mem} decides
which placements are admissible once memory is tight, and that a 7B-class
model on 8\,GB boards is already in that regime.

\subsection{Cost of Protection}\label{sec:eval:cost}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{paper/fig_protection_cost.pdf}
  \caption{Failure-free cost of protection relative to protection off (live,
  OPT-6.7B; mean and sample standard deviation over three rounds).}
  \label{fig:protection-cost}
\end{figure}

Protection is paid for during every failure-free decode step, because each
protected stage ships its new KV column to the coordinator.  We measure it on
the same placement with protection off, single parity, double parity, and
replication, three rounds each in interleaved order with ten 20-token
requests per round, all completing with identical greedy output across
modes.  Fig.~\ref{fig:protection-cost} reports the result (RQ5).  With
protection off the pipeline decodes 1.93\,tok/s with a median token interval
of 527\,ms.  Single parity changes throughput by $+0.6\pm10.7$\% and the
median token interval by $-1.4\pm9.6$\%, within the round-to-round spread
of the measurement; double parity costs $3.9\pm3.5$\% of throughput and
$3.4\pm1.7$\% of token interval; replication costs $7.6\pm2.4$\% and
$5.2\pm1.6$\%.  At three rounds the protected modes are not separated from
one another (the single-parity interval encloses the replication mean), so
these figures bound the cost rather than rank the modes.  On the 350M deployment, where these runs decode at 183\,ms per token,
the same protocol charges 5--7\% for all three modes and double parity and replication
are indistinguishable (4.906 versus 4.906\,tok/s), because both ship the
same KV columns and the second GF($2^8$) column is folded at the coordinator,
away from the workers' forward path.  As the model grows, the shipped column
grows with the layer width while the decode step grows faster with the layer
count, so the fraction of a step spent on protection shrinks.  Network
traffic itself is not an evaluation axis in this paper.

\subsection{Across Scales}\label{sec:eval:scale}

Between OPT-350M and OPT-6.7B, a $19\times$ larger model, recomputation
costs about one decode step per position at both scales (164\,ms of a
163\,ms step and $447\pm6$ of 500\,ms: 1.01 and 0.89 steps per position),
so its recovery time grows with the model only through the step;
reconfiguration grows from 24.25 to 439.4\,s; \sys{}'s slope stays within
noise at both scales (0.87 and $5.2\pm3.2$\,ms per position) and its
recovery within a few decode steps (1.6--1.9 and 2.1--3.2); its storage
advantage over replication grows from $2.25\times$ to $3.7\times$ with the
placement geometry; and the coupling induced with synthetic caps at 350M
(300--600\,MB) binds at the fleet's actual capacity at 7B.
