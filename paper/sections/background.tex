\subsection{Pipeline-parallel inference on heterogeneous edge}

Pipeline-parallel LLM inference partitions a transformer model
along its decoder-block axis: each worker hosts a contiguous range
of layers (a \emph{stage}), and an inference request flows from
stage to stage as a stream of activation tensors. Each request
carries per-stage KV cache state that the stage owner updates
in place. The two phases of generation place very different
demands on the transport: prefill ships a single
$(S \times h)$-dimensional activation through the chain ($S$ is
the prompt length, $h$ the hidden dimension), while decode pushes
a single $(1 \times h)$ activation per generated token --- only
$\sim$2\,KB at fp16 for OPT-350M's $h=1024$. The per-layer transfer
unit at decode time is two orders of magnitude smaller than at
prefill time, so the chain hop is bandwidth-cheap but
latency-binding: every token pays the round trip.

Edge heterogeneity dominates data-center heterogeneity by an order
of magnitude. On our fleet, an AGX Orin in MAXN power mode is
$\sim$16$\times$ faster per layer than a Jetson Orin Nano at
seq$=$64 CUDA decode (\S\ref{sec:eval:setup}). In a homogeneous
data-center pipeline-parallel setup, placement is a rounding
question --- whether stage 7 gets four or five layers barely
matters. On a 16$\times$-different fleet, placement is the only
thing that matters: assign two more layers to the wrong worker and
the slowest stage doubles, gating the entire chain.

Edge \emph{reliability} is also categorically worse than the data
center. During the measurement campaign behind this paper we
encountered SD-card-induced file system failures on two boards,
sustained network partitions on three, and a worker simply not
coming back from a SIGTERM on one more. A placement strategy that
treats failure as an exceptional path --- restart from scratch on
\texttt{ECONNRESET} --- produces a system whose first worker
crash aborts every in-flight stream.

\subsection{Why the obvious composition of prior systems fails}

The natural recipe is to take EdgeShard's latency-optimal DP for
$\psi$ and bolt on Petals' swarm redundancy for $R$. The
composition fails because Petals' recovery model assumes every
peer has enough free memory to hold a redundant copy of multiple
shards --- a comfortable assumption on its 16+\,GB consumer GPUs
but unaffordable on a 4\,GB Nano. The Nano is already at the layer
floor with its primary stage; reserving Petals' redundancy
displaces the primary.

A second attempt --- Jupiter's throughput-optimal DP plus a
supervisor that restarts crashed workers --- also fails, but
differently. Jupiter computes $\psi$ assuming all workers stay
alive; backup memory was never reserved at placement time, so when
a Nano dies the supervisor has nowhere to load the dead worker's
layers without OOM-ing a survivor. The system degrades to a
non-redundant chain that aborts the next failure.

These compositions miss the same systemic point: on
memory-tight edge, $\psi$ and $R$ have \emph{coupled} feasibility
regions. The set of legal placements depends on which backups are
assigned because the backup memory is part of every device's cap
(\S\ref{sec:design:dp}, Eq.~\ref{eq:mem}). Decoupling the two
problems gives up feasibility (if $R$ is solved first) or
performance (if $\psi$ is fixed before $R$ has any voice). \sys{}
is the joint solver that closes this gap.
