\section{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{sec:disc}

\textbf{When L could lose to T.} The matrix
(\S\ref{sec:eval:matrix}) shows latency placement dominates
throughput placement at every $C \geq 4$ cell on both models, but
we already see one measured exception: at $C{=}1$ on
Llama-3.2-1B, T+async beats L+async on aggregate throughput by
22\% (3.74 vs 3.05\,tok/s) while L still wins TBT p50 by 33\%
(174 vs 261\,ms). The intuition is that when a single stage's
compute time grows larger than the chain-hop cost (bigger model,
single in-flight stream), L's fast-device-heavy placement makes
that one stage the throughput-bottlenecking critical path, while
T's even division saturates wall-clock by keeping every device
busy. Multi-stream amortises the hop and L's pipeline-saturation
advantage returns at $C \geq 4$. Further regimes where T could
take the lead --- the AGX running out of memory before the Nano's
layer-floor binds, models deep enough that bulk-on-AGX overflows
(a hypothetical 7B INT4 with seven layers per Nano), bandwidth-
dominated networks --- remain extrapolations from the cost
function; we expose \texttt{optimization\_mode = blended} with a
sweepable $\alpha$ so operators can navigate the space without
re-deploying.

\textbf{Failure attribution is an explicit trade-off, not a free
choice.} Async chain forwarding buys 17--47\% additional
throughput at $C{=}16$ but loses trailer-based attribution; the
coordinator falls back to heartbeat (typically wins within $\sim$5\,s)
or the 30\,s \texttt{ResultReady} timeout (rare). Heartbeat winning
the race keeps recovery exactly as efficient as the sync path,
losing it costs one extra hop while $\psi$+$R$ feasibility
preserves correctness (all 60 tokens delivered in both regimes,
\S\ref{sec:eval:recovery}). Sync chain is the right choice when
failure attribution must be millisecond-tight (real-time
monitoring, regulatory SLOs); async chain is the right choice
when steady-state throughput dominates. The \texttt{chain\_mode}
flag is an explicit operator-facing knob, not an internal
implementation choice.

\textbf{Limitations and future work.} \sys{} treats $R$ as a
single-backup table; cascade failures (two workers in $T_R$ apart)
fall back to a degraded chain. The natural extension is
$R: D \to \mathrm{List}[D]$ with cascading promotion; the
DP recurrence extends cleanly but the memory feasibility
constraint (Eq.~\ref{eq:mem}) sums over more backed-up peers and
pushes more cells infeasible. The sharded-safetensors loader
already in production handles Llama-3.2-1B; pushing into the 7B
INT4 regime is bounded by Nano memory rather than software
(the 1B-on-Nano run in \S\ref{sec:eval:matrix} already exposes
real swap-thrash pressure). Finally, the cost function under-counts
pipeline-transition overhead by 2--4\% (\S\ref{sec:eval:a3b}); a
marginal-layer term would close the predicted-vs-measured gap.

\textbf{Conclusion.} \sys{} folds layer placement and recovery
routing into a single alternating dynamic program whose memory
feasibility check sees both decisions at once, with two runtime
mechanisms --- asynchronous mirror cache and asynchronous chain
forwarding --- that make that joint optimisation viable on a real
edge fleet. The 24-point OPT-350M matrix shows L $\succ$ T
dominance is structural to $\psi$+$R$, not architectural; a
3$\times$-larger Llama-3.2-1B sub-matrix shows the same dominance
holds at $C\geq 4$ across a model-size gap, with a measured
$C{=}1$ cross-over that bounds the claim cleanly. Async chain
adds 17--47\% throughput at $C{=}16$ without ever reversing the
placement ordering at $C\geq 4$, confirming that placement and
chain mode are orthogonal knobs for the operator to navigate.
