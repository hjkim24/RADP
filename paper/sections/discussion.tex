\section{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{sec:disc}

\subsection{Discussion}\label{sec:disc:discussion}

\textbf{Parity against replication.}  The two zero-recomputation paths
recover in the same time, and DejaVu-style replication has the lower
intercept (239 versus 284\,ms) because it installs one stored column instead
of fetching three surviving ones.  The argument for parity is therefore
retained state, and it holds when that state matters: long contexts, larger
models, or a coordinator whose memory is itself constrained.  With short
requests and a coordinator that has memory to spare, replication is the
simpler choice, and \sys{}'s \texttt{ReplicaCache} path provides it on the
same shipping channel.  Which of the two is appropriate follows from the
per-token footprint and the context length, both of which the coordinator
knows before deployment.

\textbf{Reactive reconfiguration.}  Reconfigure's median of 24.25\,s is
dominated by re-solving placement, cold-loading weights on the newly assigned
workers, and rebuilding the pipeline; replaying the prefix accounts for
1.6--6.4\,s of it.  Because the surviving fleet, and hence the victim,
differs from trial to trial, we report level and range rather than a slope.
This baseline is deliberately unassisted.  SpotServe reaches much lower
migration cost by planning around advance preemption notice and a grace
period~\cite{miao2024spotserve}; an unannounced crash or out-of-memory kill on
an edge worker offers neither, which is why \sys{} keeps its backup weights
loaded and its recovery state current before the failure.

\textbf{Two simultaneous failures.}  The double-parity result should be read
for its guarantee and its slope: all five two-failure trials reconstructed
both lost columns from $P$ and $Q$, and the fitted slope of 2.78\,ms per
position places it in the same flat band as single parity.  Its 30.3\,s
intercept reflects the recovery mapping that the scheduler built for this
fleet, which assigns every non-head backup to one Orin Nano; a second
promotion on that board is a cold weight load.  A mapping that spreads the
backups over different hosts removes that cost: at 7B scale, where the
scheduler's mapping lands the two backups on distinct nodes, the $k=2$
intercept is 0.58\,s (Section~\ref{sec:eval:scale}).

\textbf{Fidelity of the recovered KV.}  Parity and replication install the
bytes that were stored, so the recovered KV equals what the failed stage held.
Replay regenerates it through a forward pass, and on heterogeneous hardware
the regenerated bytes differ from the original: the same fp16 input through
the same stage on a CUDA and a CPU worker disagreed in 26.9\% of KV elements,
with differences up to $2^{-8}$ (Section~\ref{sec:eval:fidelity}).  We present
this as a property of the recovered state.  A parity recovery that completes
its contract is deterministic with respect to the pre-failure state; a replay
recovery is only as reproducible as the pair of devices involved.

\textbf{When placement and recovery couple.}  With 5--25\,GB free per device,
both the cost-first and the joint procedure find a backup for OPT-350M, so
none of the live deployments in this paper demonstrates coupling.  The
feasibility result is a statement about the memory-constrained regime: once
the free memory per device approaches the size of a stage and its backup, the
reservation in Eq.~\ref{eq:mem} decides which placements are admissible, and
the alternating search of Section~\ref{sec:design:dp} finds placements that a sequential procedure
discards.  Where the threshold lies depends on which devices may host
backups; widening that set to nodes outside the pipeline moved it from
600\,MB to 200\,MB on our fleet.

\textbf{Limitations.}  The measurements come from one fleet of five CUDA
workers and two CPU-only nodes and from one model, OPT-350M; larger models
and other device classes remain to be measured.  The protection domain covers
fail-stop failures of non-head workers: the head stage and the coordinator
are unprotected, a last-stage failure falls back to replay because no
downstream survivor can confirm the final slot, and a failure during prefill
has no prefix to reconstruct.  Latency was measured with synchronous
forwarding; asynchronous forwarding adds a heartbeat-bounded detection delay
common to all families that we did not quantify, and a worker that hangs
rather than crashes can still be misattributed.  The fidelity probe covers
one tier pair and one two-layer stage.  The storage advantage depends on
placement geometry: on this head-heavy placement double parity saves only
11\% over replication, and a third parity column would cost more state than
replication while tolerating fewer failures.  Each recovery-latency point is
one trial; the protection-cost figures are means over three rounds.  Network
traffic is not an evaluation axis in this paper.

\textbf{Future work.}  Measuring double parity under a backup mapping spread
over distinct hosts, and Reconfigure with a fixed victim, would complete the
latency comparison.  Larger models, fleets with genuinely different SKUs, and
a head/middle/tail victim sweep would test the generality of the flat-in-$P$
result.  Protecting the coordinator, by replicating parity state or by pairing
coordinators, and covering the head stage are the next steps for the
protection domain.  A general $k$-parity construction with Cauchy coefficients
becomes worthwhile only on pipelines whose non-head stages are many and
balanced, that is, when $\sum_s\ell_s/\ell_{\max}$ is large.  Finally, the
recovery contract can be relaxed for tail victims and prefill failures, and
the placement objective can be extended with recovery latency and with the
per-hop and stage-count costs that the current cost model approximates.

\subsection{Conclusion}\label{sec:disc:conclusion}

IoT deployments that must keep inference on site run LLMs as pipelines over
heterogeneous, memory-constrained devices, and a stage failure in such a
pipeline loses the request's KV state.  Existing recovery paths make an
operator choose between recomputation whose cost grows with the prefix,
replication whose state grows with the sum of the stages, and reconfiguration
that takes tens of seconds.  \sys{} recovers the lost KV from one or two
cross-stage parity columns without recomputing the prefix, and it selects the
layer placement together with the backup assignment so that the recovered
state has a device to run on.  On a five-stage Jetson pipeline, single parity
recovers in 0.30--0.32\,s regardless of the failure position, retains
$2.25\times$ less state than replication, and costs 5--7\% of failure-free
throughput; double parity restores two simultaneous stage failures from the
same stored bytes; and joint placement remains feasible at per-device memory
caps of 300--600\,MB where a cost-first placement cannot be backed up.  For
edge deployments that cannot depend on a remote service, this turns a stage
failure from a request loss or a multi-second stall into a delay of about two
decode steps.
