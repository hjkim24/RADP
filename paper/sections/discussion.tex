\section{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{sec:disc}

\subsection{Discussion}\label{sec:disc:discussion}

\textbf{Parity against replication.}  The two zero-recomputation paths
have the same flat shape, and DejaVu-style replication has the lower
intercept at both scales (0.95 versus 1.23\,s at 7B, 239 versus 284\,ms at
350M) because it installs one stored column instead of fetching the surviving
ones.  The argument for parity is therefore
retained state, and it holds when that state matters: long contexts, larger
models, or a coordinator whose memory is itself constrained.  With short
requests and a coordinator that has memory to spare, replication is the
simpler choice, and \sys{}'s \texttt{ReplicaCache} path provides it on the
same shipping channel.  Which of the two is appropriate follows from the
per-token footprint and the context length, both of which the coordinator
knows before deployment.

\textbf{Reactive reconfiguration.}  Reconfigure's median of 439.4\,s at 7B
and 24.25\,s at 350M is dominated by re-profiling the survivors, re-solving
placement, cold-loading weights on the newly assigned workers, and rebuilding
the pipeline; replaying the prefix accounts for 1.6--6.4\,s of the 350M
figure.  Because the surviving fleet, and hence the victim, differs from
trial to trial, we report level and range rather than a slope; at 7B two
positions were measured, and the five-position characterization of this
baseline is the 350M one.  This baseline is deliberately unassisted.  SpotServe reaches much lower
migration cost by planning around advance preemption notice and a grace
period~\cite{miao2024spotserve}; an unannounced crash or out-of-memory kill on
an edge worker offers neither, which is why \sys{} keeps its backup weights
loaded and its recovery state current before the failure.

\textbf{Two simultaneous failures.}  The double-parity result should be read
for its guarantee first: at both scales every two-failure trial reconstructed
both lost columns from $P$ and $Q$.  Its absolute cost depends on the recovery mapping.  With the two backups on
distinct nodes, as the scheduler placed them at 7B, recovery completes in
1.1--1.4\,s, the same as a single failure; with every non-head backup on one Orin Nano, as on the 350M
placement, the second promotion is a cold weight load and the intercept
rises to 30.3\,s while the slope stays flat.  A scheduler that spreads
backups therefore buys double-failure tolerance at roughly the cost of
single-failure recovery.

\textbf{Fidelity of the recovered KV.}  Parity and replication install the
bytes that were stored, so the recovered KV equals what the failed stage held.
Replay regenerates it through a forward pass, and on heterogeneous hardware
the regenerated bytes differ from the original: the same fp16 input through
the same stage on a CUDA and a CPU worker disagreed in 26.9\% of KV elements,
with differences up to $2^{-8}$ (Section~\ref{sec:eval:fidelity}).  We present
this as a property of the recovered state.  A parity recovery that completes
its contract is deterministic with respect to the pre-failure state; a replay
recovery is only as reproducible as the pair of devices involved.

\textbf{When placement and recovery couple.}  Coupling is a property of the
memory-constrained regime: once the free memory per device approaches the
size of a stage and its backup, the reservation in Eq.~\ref{eq:mem} decides
which placements are admissible, and the alternating search of
Section~\ref{sec:design:dp} finds placements that a sequential procedure
discards.  The 350M deployment reaches that regime only under synthetic
memory caps, whereas the 7B deployment lives in it: the live solve rejected a
quarter of its candidate placements for want of backup room, and the
cost-first procedure has no feasible placement at any cap at which the model
fits.  Where the threshold lies depends on which devices may host backups;
allowing backups on devices outside the pipeline dissolved the gap on both
fleets.

\textbf{Limitations.}  The measurements come from one fleet of Jetson boards
and from two models of one family, OPT-6.7B and OPT-350M; other model
families and device classes remain to be measured.  At 7B, Reconfigure was
measured at two positions.  The protection domain covers
fail-stop failures of non-head workers: the head stage and the coordinator
are unprotected, a last-stage failure falls back to replay because no
downstream survivor can confirm the final slot, and a failure during prefill
has no prefix to reconstruct.  The injected failure is a deterministic exception raised after the victim's
input for position $P$ has reached the coordinator, the case the recovery
contract is designed for; process kills, out-of-memory terminations, failures
that strike before the mirrored input lands (contract check (iv)), and the
latency of the replay fallback they trigger were not measured, so the
reported latencies characterize the parity path rather than the average over
all failure timings.  Latency was measured with
synchronous forwarding; asynchronous forwarding adds a heartbeat-bounded
detection delay common to all families that we did not quantify, and a
worker that hangs rather than crashes can still be misattributed.  Backup weights are preloaded and Eq.~\ref{eq:mem} reserves memory for them;
a lazy variant that loads only the failed stage's weights at failure time
would relax the reservation at a recovery cost between \sys{} and
Reconfigure, a middle point we did not measure.  The coordinator holds all
parity state and is itself unprotected; its memory grows with concurrent
requests and context length beyond the per-request figures above.  The
scheduler does not optimize where backups land, so the two-failure result
holds for mappings that, like the 7B one, place the two backups on distinct
nodes.  The fidelity probe covers
one tier pair and one two-layer stage on the 350M deployment.  The storage
advantage depends on placement geometry: double parity saves 46\% over
replication on the 7B placement but only 11\% on the head-heavy 350M
placement, where a third parity column would cost more state than
replication while tolerating fewer failures.  Each recovery-latency fit
pools three trials per position; the victim-sensitivity sweep and the 350M
sweeps are one trial per position; the protection-cost figures are means over
three rounds.  Network
traffic is not an evaluation axis in this paper.

\textbf{Future work.}  A full Reconfigure sweep at 7B, end-to-end
measurements that include heartbeat detection, process-kill injection, and
the lazy-backup middle point would complete the latency comparison, and
adding backup spread to the placement objective would make the two-failure
cost independent of where backups land.  Other model families, fleets with
different SKUs, and a head/middle/tail victim sweep would test the
generality of the flat-in-$P$ result.  Protecting the coordinator and the
head stage are the next steps for the protection domain; a general
$k$-parity construction with Cauchy coefficients pays off only when
$\sum_s\ell_s/\ell_{\max}$ is large; and the contract can be relaxed for
tail victims and prefill failures while the placement objective absorbs
recovery latency and per-hop costs.

\subsection{Conclusion}\label{sec:disc:conclusion}

IoT deployments that keep inference on site run LLMs as pipelines over
heterogeneous, memory-constrained devices, where a stage failure loses the
request's KV state and existing recovery paths trade prefix-length
recomputation, sum-of-stages replication, or minutes of reconfiguration.
\sys{} recovers the lost KV from one or two cross-stage parity columns
without recomputing the prefix and selects the layer placement together with
the backup assignment so that the recovered state has a device to run on.  On a six-stage Jetson pipeline serving OPT-6.7B, single parity
recovers in 1.2--1.4\,s regardless of the failure position while
recomputation grows to 16\,s, retains $3.7\times$ less state than
replication, and costs no measurable failure-free throughput; double parity
restores two simultaneous stage failures from the same stored bytes in about
a second; and joint placement remains feasible at every per-device memory cap
at which the model fits, where a cost-first placement cannot be backed up.
For edge deployments that cannot depend on a remote service, this turns a
stage failure from a request loss or a minutes-long reconfiguration into a
delay of two to three decode steps.
